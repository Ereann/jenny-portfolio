I’m a Melbourne-based designer and illustrator with a Bachelor of Communication Design from RMIT University. My practice is driven by personality and emotion, using design as a way to translate feeling into visual form.

I love creating visuals that feel lived-in, textured and expressive. I approach each project with the belief that design is most impactful when it reflects the stories and context behind it, ensuring each outcome feels authentic and intentional.

Exploration
Soft studies · printed moods · music worlds

a quiet notebook of experiments

Small visual worlds shaped by sound, texture, memory and atmosphere.

These are not separate services or heavy case-study blocks. They are softer entries into Jenny’s visual practice — small clickable notes that open into stories about how the work begins, drifts, changes and becomes a finished mood.

entry 01 · identity studies

Visual identities that feel handmade, slightly off-centre and emotionally specific.

Jenny’s identity work often starts before there is a logo. It begins with a feeling: the colour of a room, the softness of packaging paper, the way a sign might look under afternoon light, or how a tiny illustrated character can make a brand feel less distant.

In projects like Little Picco’s and Soul Tides, the identity becomes a small world. The marks, posters, menus and objects are not treated as separate outcomes — they are fragments of the same atmosphere.

Note from the process

I like when a brand feels as if someone has touched every part of it gently. Not perfect, not over-polished, but considered. For these identity studies, I kept returning to small details: a rounded corner, a strange little face, a soft colour that almost disappears.

The work becomes interesting when it starts to feel lived in. I want the viewer to imagine the place before they enter it — the smell of bread, the sound of a record, the quiet pause before someone picks up a printed card.

entry 02 · music worlds

Design language for songs that feel blurred at the edges.

This direction leans into shoegaze: washed contrast, dim light, grain, repetition and slow emotional build. The work avoids loud music graphics and instead uses atmosphere, negative space and fragile typography to suggest sound.

After Midnight, Dottie and Soul Tides sit in this space — posters, tour pieces, vinyl-bar moments and visual systems that feel like walking home after a gig with your ears still ringing.

Writing through sound

I think music-led design should feel like a memory of the song, not a literal poster for it. I kept thinking about distortion as something soft, not aggressive — a layer of haze over text, a photograph that feels slightly lost, a composition that lets silence sit inside it.

The shoegaze feeling comes through in the pacing. Nothing needs to shout. The type can be thin, the contrast can be low, and the image can almost fade away. That almost-disappearing quality is where the emotion sits.

entry 03 · printed objects

Objects that feel collected, kept and found again later.

Print gives the portfolio its physical softness. These explorations look at how postcards, labels, stamps, menus and merch can hold a story without needing to explain too much.

The work uses paper grain, small margins, soft illustration and muted contrast to make each object feel like it belongs to a private archive.

Things people keep

I’m drawn to design that can be held. A card, a ticket, a menu or a label can become a souvenir even when it was only meant to be useful. The printed object carries the memory of where it came from.

For these pieces, I wanted the work to feel quiet enough to sit in a drawer, between books, or pinned near a desk. The edges, spacing and paper-like textures matter because they make the design feel less like a screen and more like a trace.

entry 04 · editorial atmospheres

Layouts that move slowly, with room for silence.

The editorial work focuses on pacing: image to text, blank space to density, small details to full-page atmosphere. Rather than feeling polished in a commercial way, the layouts stay quiet and cinematic.

These studies connect Jenny’s print, identity and music-led projects by showing how composition can create a mood before a viewer reads a single word.

On pacing a page

When I work with layout, I think about rhythm more than decoration. Some pages need to feel like a held breath. Some need repetition. Some need one small image sitting in a wide empty field so the viewer slows down.

The quiet space is not empty to me. It is where the work starts to sound different — like the pause between tracks, or the last light in a room after the noise has stopped.

entry 05 · packaging moments

Packaging treated as a tiny scene, not just a container.

These studies explore the emotional side of packaging: what someone sees first, what they hold, what they notice after a second look. The design is simple, but the details carry personality.

Across café and product-style work, Jenny uses repetition, soft colour, little symbols and character-led marks to make everyday objects feel more personal.

Small rituals

I like packaging when it becomes part of a routine: opening something, folding it, carrying it, keeping it for no clear reason. The design has to work quickly, but it can still leave behind a small feeling.

For Little Picco’s, the packaging is playful without becoming too loud. The character details and softened colours are there to make the object feel familiar, like something you would want to pick up again.

entry 06 · type experiments

Typography that feels like a voice coming through a wall.

Jenny’s type experiments move between delicate serif forms, expressive scripts and clean supporting systems. The aim is not to make type decorative, but to make it hold tone: nostalgic, strange, soft, romantic or slightly distorted.

This is where the shoegaze influence becomes visual — letters as atmosphere, hierarchy as volume, spacing as reverb.

Letters as atmosphere

Sometimes the type needs to feel close and readable. Sometimes it should feel distant, like it is arriving through a layer of noise. I like testing how far a word can be softened before it stops communicating and starts becoming mood.

The scripts, serifs and narrow supporting type create a contrast that feels romantic but slightly unstable. That tension is important — it keeps the work from becoming too neat.
